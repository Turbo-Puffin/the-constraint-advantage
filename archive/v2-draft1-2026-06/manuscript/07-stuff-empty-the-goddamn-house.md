# 7. Stuff: empty the goddamn house.

Walk through your house tonight, after everyone's asleep, and count the things that are waiting for you.

Not the chores. The *things*. The drawer that doesn't close because of what's in it. The garage shelf with the project that's been "almost done" since the previous administration. The bin of cables for devices you no longer own, kept against the day a 2011 camcorder rises from the dead and demands its charger. The exercise equipment that transitioned, gracefully, into sculpture.

Every one of those objects is making a small claim on you. Each says: deal with me, eventually. Maintain me. Store me. Move me when you move. Feel a microgram of guilt when your eyes pass over me. One object's claim is nothing. You own — and I want you to sit with this number, because the researchers who count such things put the average American household around it — three hundred thousand objects.

You don't own a home. You operate a regional distribution center with a kitchen in it.

## A full house is a full head.

Here's the thing I had to learn before any of the purging made sense: clutter isn't a storage problem. It's a *cognitive* problem wearing a storage costume.

Every object in your line of sight is a tiny open browser tab. The unfinished project isn't sitting on the workbench — it's sitting in your head, running a background process called *you should really.* The full closet runs one called *someday.* The garage runs so many processes it's basically mining bitcoin. You walk through your own house at the end of a long day and instead of restoring you, the house *bills* you — a little attention here, a little guilt there, room after room. Men think they want a bigger house. What they want is a quieter one, and square footage makes it louder.

And notice — chapter 2 alumni will see this coming — *how the stuff got there.* The stuff is the fossil record of the lie of more. The upgrade cycle, the hobby gear bought in the enthusiasm phase, the aspirational purchases (the kayak: a prayer in plastic), the deal too good to pass up, which is how you pay to store other people's inventory. You worked the long hours to afford things you now maintain on weekends, and the things have one more joke in them: they made the house — the place the long hours were *for* — worse at its one job, which was being the place you could rest.

## The purge.

So my wife and I are emptying the house. Present tense, deliberately — this isn't a weekend stunt with before-and-after photos. It's a running project, room by room, bin by bin, and it's been one of the stranger and better things we've done together. **[STORY SLOTS, the real texture goes here: the room that started it · the hardest category · the best single fight · the find that stopped you both cold.]**

Here's what nobody tells you about purging a family home: the house is an archive of arguments you never finished and selves you never became. That's why it's hard. You're not deciding about an object; you're deciding about the version of you that bought it. The golf clubs aren't clubs, they're a referendum on whether you're still the guy who was going to golf. Letting go of the object means closing the case on that guy, and it turns out a lot of us are storing entire *cold-case departments* in the basement, climate-controlled, just in case.

Close the cases. Here's the operating manual we've settled into, and notice it's just this book's machinery pointed at the garage:

**Subtraction over organization.** The container-store move — buying stuff to manage your stuff — is the lie of more with a label maker; a beautifully organized bin of things you never use is still a monument to never-use, just easier to dust. **Constraints over willpower:** never "declutter more" (a resolution, doomed) but a rule — one room a month; nothing new enters without something leaving; if it's been boxed since the last house, the box doesn't get a vote. **And do it together.** This is the part I'd underline twice. Done solo it's spring cleaning; done as a couple it's something else — you're renegotiating, object by object, what your family's one finite life is actually for. We've had conversations over a donation pile that we hadn't had in years. Some were about the pile. The good ones weren't.

And the kids watch, of course. They watch everything — chapter 16 looms — and a kid who sees his parents cheerfully carry boxes *out* of the house is receiving a piece of information almost no American child gets: that the inflow is optional. That stuff is a decision. One unboxing video on the internet versus a dad who owns one good hammer and knows where it is — I know which lesson has a shot at outliving me. **[True kid-and-the-purge anecdote slot — there's always one.]**

## The room that came back.

I'll tell you how you know it's working, and it's not the donation receipts.

It's the first room that goes *quiet*. **[Real room goes here — which one was it?]** One day a room in your house finishes — surfaces clear, every object in it either used or loved, nothing waiting, nothing pending — and you walk in and feel the difference in your chest before you can name it. The room stopped billing you. It became a *place* instead of a to-do list with walls. You'll catch yourself going in there for no reason, the way you'd visit a friend.

Then somebody reads in it. Somebody's homework migrates to it. The room rejoins the family — recovered territory, and your evenings are suddenly happening *in* it. That's the dividend. Not minimalism; nobody in this book is counting their possessions down to a number with a blog post attached. Just a house doing its one job again: holding the people, instead of the inventory between them.

One move. Not the garage — the garage is the boss level; you'll die there and never restart. One drawer. Tonight. The junk drawer, the one that fights back when you open it. Empty it onto the counter, put back the five things that have a job, and let the rest go.

Then open and close it a few times, just to feel it.

That click is the sound of one tab closing. There's a whole house of them, and now you know.
