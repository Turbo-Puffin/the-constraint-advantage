# 10. People: shrink the circle.

Somewhere on your phone right now is a group text you're afraid to leave.

You know the one. It's been running since 2019. It produces forty messages a day, of which you read four, none of which required reading. Leaving it would be *noticed* — there'd be a screenshot, a "everything ok bro?" — so you stay, the way you'd stay in a timeshare. You are, at this moment in your life, a dues-paying member of perhaps a dozen social arrangements you would not join again if offered a clean slate.

That's this chapter. We've cleared the house and the calendar; now we do the hardest closet in the building — the one with people in it.

## The social junk drawer.

Be honest about the inventory. The standing poker night that stopped being fun two strollers ago but survives on institutional momentum. The couple-friends you see quarterly out of pure mutual guilt — dinner as debt service, each side secretly relieved at every reschedule (the modern handshake: "we should have you guys over!" — a sentence that means nothing, signed by no one). The neighborhood thing you attend so nobody talks. The college guys whose entire friendship now runs on referencing the same four stories from 1998, a museum you visit where the exhibits never change.

None of these people are bad people. That's what makes it hard — the closet is full of perfectly good shirts. But chapter 7's rule holds: *perfectly good* is not the bar. The bar is whether it's *yours* — current you, would-choose-it-again you. And the arithmetic underneath is the book's oldest, meanest math: every obligation-evening is withdrawn from the same tiny account as everything else you love. The sliver, chapter 9 called it. There are maybe eight discretionary evenings in a man's month. The junk drawer is holding five of them.

Here's the tell, and it's reliable: real friendship leaves you *fuller* than it found you. Obligation leaves you checking your watch in the driveway, doing the math on the babysitter, feeling — and men never say this part out loud — *relieved to be heading home.* Track that feeling for a month. The driveway doesn't lie.

## Fewer, deeper, weirder.

So: shrink it. On purpose. Here's what the constraint buys, because this chapter isn't anti-friend — it's the opposite. It's anti-*acquaintance-theater*, in defense of the real thing.

A man needs a small number of 2 a.m. friends — the ones you could call from a hospital parking lot, the ones who'd start driving before you finished the sentence. **[STORY SLOT: Matt's 2 a.m. friend — or the parking lot where he learned who his was.]** Everybody knows the term the second they hear it, and everybody starts counting, and the count is usually two or three, and that's *correct* — that's what the count is supposed to be. The 2 a.m. friendship doesn't scale. It runs on accumulated specific knowledge of one particular man, ten thousand hours of him, and you cannot index that across a dozen group chats. The acquaintance portfolio isn't *diversifying* your friendships. It's diluting them — spreading 2 a.m. fuel across forty guys who are all, structurally, 2 *p.m.* friends.

The move is the same as every chapter: subtraction first, then watch what the cleared ground does. Decline the quarterly guilt dinner — just don't rebook it; let it die its natural death, no announcement (chapter 4's rule: God spare everyone the announcement). Mute the group text; leave it in January when the membership rolls over and nobody audits. Resign the committee. And then — this is the part men skip — *spend the recovered evenings on the short list.* Fewer, deeper, and yes, weirder: the friendships that survive the cut tend to be the strange, specific ones, the guy you talk to about God and smokers, not the guy you talk to about traffic. Depth has a flavor. Breadth all tastes the same.

Then put a fence around what's left. In our house it's the standing thing — same people, recurring, defended like the Saturday from chapter 8. **[STORY SLOT: the real standing thing — the Sunday, the camp weekend, whatever it is.]** A standing commitment to actual friends is a constraint (decided once, runs without you, survives tired-you's flakiness) and it does for friendship what the fixed budget did for money: ends the relitigating. Nobody's coordinating six calendars every time. It's Sunday. We'll be there. That sentence, sustained for a decade, is worth more than every networking event ever held.

## The doorbell curriculum.

And of course they're watching. You knew this paragraph was coming.

Your kids are not learning friendship from your advice. They're learning it from the *traffic* — which doorbells you answer, which calls you take in the other room with the door closed because it's Mike and it's real, versus which invitations make you sigh at the counter before typing "sounds great!" They're building their entire model of what adult connection looks like from the data set you generate in the kitchen.

So consider what the overstuffed circle teaches: that friendship is mostly performance and dread. That grown men spend their rare free evenings with people who leave them empty, because exiting would be awkward. That *awkward* outranks *alive*. Now consider the alternative curriculum: a dad with three real friends who shows up for them like it's a covenant, who is visibly, audibly *glad* on the porch. A kid who grows up watching that doesn't need the lecture about choosing friends. He's been to the seminar. It met on Sundays.

One move this month — one in, one out. **Out:** pick the single obligation on the social calendar that you'd pay actual money to skip, and skip it. No replacement, no eulogy. **In:** take the recovered evening and aim it at the top of the short list — the 2 a.m. friend you haven't seen in too long. Steaks, garage, whatever the liturgy is.

You'll come home from one of those evenings fuller than you left. Driveway test. The driveway doesn't lie.
