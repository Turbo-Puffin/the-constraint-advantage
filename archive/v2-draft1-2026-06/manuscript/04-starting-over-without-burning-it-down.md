# 4. Starting over without burning it down.

Every overloaded man I know has the same fantasy, and it's not the one his wife worries about.

It's the cabin.

You know the cabin. You've built it in your head during conference calls. It's somewhere with pine trees. There's a wood stove. There's no Wi-Fi, or there is but you're very disciplined about it. In the fantasy you've quit the job, sold the house, liquidated the whole catastrophe, and you're finally living *simply* — you, an axe, and a level of self-regard that should require a permit.

The cabin fantasy feels like the opposite of your overstuffed life. I want to show you it's the same disease.

## Torching it is just "more," inverted.

Look at the actual mechanics of the fantasy. What is it? It's a man solving overload with a *dramatic transaction*. Everything must go. New identity, new geography, new wardrobe (flannel, inexplicably — the uniform of a man who has replaced one costume with another). It's maximalism wearing camouflage: maximum gesture, maximum disruption, maximum story to tell at the cookout.

The quiet tell is who pays for it. The cabin fantasy always edits the family down to a supporting role or out of the frame entirely, which should bother you more than it does. Because the people in your house were never the problem. The mortgage isn't the problem. The town isn't the problem. The problem is the *rules* you've been running — every input set to yes, every default set to more — and rules travel. Move a man with broken defaults to a cabin and you get a cabin with seventeen unfinished projects and a guy checking his phone by the wood stove.

You can't geography your way out of an operating system.

And the burn-it-down move fails in the other direction too, the one the minimalism books are weirdly quiet about. The guys who actually do it — sell everything, fit their lives in a backpack, post about it — notice how they're 26? A backpack is easy when nobody's in it. Try fitting a twelve-year-old's science fair project in a backpack. The literature of starting over is written almost entirely by men with nothing to carry, which makes it worthless to you, because the whole question of your life is that you're carrying people you love.

So the real question isn't "how do I escape?" The real question — the one this chapter exists for — is: **can a man with a full house start over without emptying it?**

Yes. I'm proof, and I did it at an age you might find encouraging.

## Starting over in place.

Here's what I've learned about reinvention, having done it more than once: the dramatic version is overrated and the boring version is criminally underrated.

I've changed my working life completely a few times now. Agency guy, then public servant, then a guy who runs his own small things on his own terms. From the outside, those look like clean breaks. From the inside, not one of them required burning anything. The house stayed. The marriage stayed. The kids kept their schools. What changed was the *rules* — which is to say, what changed was what I said yes to, and the entire infrastructure of my life came along intact.

That's the move. **Starting over in place.** Same address, different defaults.

And the mechanism — you knew this was coming — is constraints. A constraint is how you start over without the bonfire. You don't quit money; you fix the budget. You don't sell the house; you empty the junk out of it. You don't abandon the career; you put walls around it. You don't delete the phone; you give it a bedtime like the other children. Each limit is a small, reversible, undramatic act of starting over. Nobody at the cookout will gasp. There's no flannel. It works anyway.

There's even a quiet advantage in it, one the cabin guys lose: when you start over in place, you keep your *witnesses*. The same people who watched you live the loud way get to watch you live the quiet way. That's not embarrassing. That's the most credible thing a man can do in front of his kids. More on that in chapter 16, and it might be the most important argument in this book.

## Even at 49.

Now the age thing, because I know it's in the room.

There's a voice that says: too late. The voice has data, even. The kids are already mostly grown, the habits are decades deep, the career is what it is. Starting over is for the 26-year-old with the backpack. You had your window.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is what kills that voice: **the years you spent thinking it was too late were themselves the cost.** That's the trap's whole design. "Too late" feels like a conclusion, but it functions as a sedative — it's the thought that lets you stay overloaded one more year, and the years compound either way.

Run the actual numbers instead, since we like numbers here. A man at 49 has, actuarially, around thirty-some years left — and say his kid's got six more at home. The "too late" voice wants you to grieve the years you missed. Fine, grieve them; chapter 1 was me doing exactly that, and the grief is load-bearing. But six years is over three hundred Saturdays still on the books. Three hundred. A man who walked into a casino with three hundred chips would not describe himself as cleaned out — and the thirty-some years after that are when *your kids decide what you were like,* by the way. The verdict isn't in. You're still in the part of the story where the dad can change, which happens to be everyone's favorite part of the story.

You don't have to be young to start over. You have to be *willing to be a beginner*, which is harder at our age, frankly, because we've spent decades getting good at things and beginner feels like demotion. It isn't. A 49-year-old beginner has something the backpack kid would kill for: he knows exactly what everything costs, because he's been paying full price for years.

So no bonfire. No cabin. No announcement, even — God spare your family the announcement.

One move: pick the smallest rule you'd change if you were starting your life today, knowing what you know. Not the biggest. The smallest. A phone that sleeps in the kitchen. A Saturday morning with a fence around it. One standing thing on the calendar, deleted with a clear conscience.

Change that one rule, in place, this week, and tell no one.

Congratulations. You've started over. It looked like nothing. They all do.
