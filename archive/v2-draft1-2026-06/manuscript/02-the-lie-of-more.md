# 2. The lie of more.

Somewhere along the way you signed a contract you never read.

The terms were roughly: a good father provides. Providing is measured in volume. Therefore the hours, the title, the second project, the side thing, the yes, the yes, the yes — all of it counts as love, payable on some future date when things calm down.

Nobody hands you this contract. It assembles itself out of ambient material: your own dad, who worked like a rented mule and called it devotion. Every movie where the redemption arc is a man finally making it to the game in the third act (notice he was *allowed* to miss the first two). LinkedIn, where grown men post about 4 a.m. like the sun gives out trophies. The general atmosphere of American fatherhood, in which "he's a hard worker" is the highest compliment a man can receive at his own funeral, by which point it is not useful information.

I signed it. Both hands. I can prove it, because I wrote the receipts down while it was happening.

---

In 2015 I described my working life, in print, as "burnout by a thousand cuts." I was in consulting then — five years at an agency, worked my way up from consultant to VP. Good firm, real clients, real work: financial companies, race teams, retailers you've heard of. From the outside it was a career going *extremely well.*

Here's what it was from the inside: a thousand small fights I kept losing on schedule. You fight for the right thing all project and compromise at the end, every project, every time, because in agency work the deadline is the actual product. The client buys on-time. Whether the thing is *good* is a bonus feature. So you trim, and ship, and tell yourself next project, and the next project arrives pre-trimmed.

And what does a competent man do when the work eats him? He concludes he's not working hard enough, obviously. More hours. More fights, lost slower. I climbed the whole ladder like that — and a ladder is a great place to spend a decade if your goal is to not be home.

Because that's the clause they don't read you. The lie of more has a payment schedule, and you don't pay it. They do. Every yes you give at work is a no you mail home, and you don't even feel yourself sending it. The yes happens in a conference room where everyone congratulates you. The no arrives at a dinner table, quietly, where someone shorter than you eats the cost and doesn't have the vocabulary to invoice you for it.

So they don't invoice you. They adapt. That's the worst part — kids are *flexible*. They mold themselves around your absence like a tree growing around a chain-link fence. It looks like resilience. It photographs fine. And years later you realize the shape of the tree *is* the fence, and you're the fence.

---

Now, the obvious objection, because you're already making it: "Money's real. The mortgage is real. I can't love them in a tent."

Correct. This book is not going to tell you money doesn't matter; it's going to keep telling you money matters, with numbers, because I find men relax once they know the author can count. The mortgage is real.

But run the actual math on your last few years, the way you'd run it on a business. The marginal hours — hour fifty-one through hour sixty-five — what did they buy? Not hour one through forty; those pay the mortgage. The *extra*. The evenings, the Sunday emails, the project you took on top of your job because it "opens doors." Price what it actually purchased: some percentage raise, maybe. A title with one more word in it. And now price what it cost, except you can't, because the things in the cost column don't have prices. That's not sentiment — that's just what *irreplaceable* means. It's the economic term for your daughter at seven. There is no marketplace where you can buy back a Tuesday from when she was seven, at any wage, and you knew that, and you traded the Tuesday anyway, because the lie of more does its bookkeeping in a currency it never has to pay out.

More is a pyramid scheme where you recruit yourself.

*(A sidebar, for the skeptics, from my own résumé: I once worked a job with a hard seven-hour day. Union shop, municipal government — the clock was the clock. Conference-circuit guys would call that a productivity disaster. It was the most productive environment I've ever worked in, and I wrote that sentence in 2018 and I'll stand on it today: you can get more done in seven hours on a full night's sleep than in a week of burning the midnight oil. Hold that thought; the whole back half of this book is built on it.)*

---

Here's the reframe this chapter exists to install, and then I'll let you go.

Providing isn't a volume business. It never was. Ask the people you provide for — actually ask them, it's a vertigo-inducing experiment. Nobody in your house is keeping the metrics you're killing yourself against. There is no scoreboard on the refrigerator tracking your utilization. What they measure — and they measure it constantly, with the cold precision of auditors — is something your time sheet doesn't capture: *did you look up.*

A good father provides. Sure. But you are not just providing revenue. You're providing the answer to a question your kids are quietly asking every single day, which is: *what does a man's life look like?* And if your answer is "tired, important, and elsewhere" — that's the inheritance. That's what got provided.

The lie of more says: they'll understand someday.
The truth is worse. They will. They'll understand it perfectly, in their thirties, in their own kitchens, holding their own phones. *(Chapter 16 is about that, and I needed a few chapters to work up to it.)*

One move before you go. Tonight, no spreadsheet required: name the last thing you did *for the family* that the family didn't want. The extra project "for them." The weekend work "for them." Say it out loud with the "for them" attached and see if it survives contact with the air.

Most of mine didn't.
