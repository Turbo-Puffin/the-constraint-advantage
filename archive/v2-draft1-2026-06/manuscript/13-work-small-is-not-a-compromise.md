# 13. Work: small is not a compromise.

We've saved the big one for last in this part, because work is where the lie of more keeps its head office.

Everything else this book has asked you to shrink — the stuff, the calendar, the circle — you were at least allowed to *want* to shrink. But work? Work has ambition guarding the door. Suggest a man do less at work and you're not questioning his schedule, you're questioning his trajectory, his provider credentials, his *story*. Careers are supposed to go up and to the right. Smaller is the direction failures go.

I want to spend this chapter dismantling that, and I get to use my own résumé as the wrecking ball.

## The ladder and the cliff.

I climbed the ladder properly. Five years at a consulting agency: started as a principal consultant, worked my way up to VP. Real clients — financial giants, retailers you've shopped at, a race team, which remains the best answer I've ever had to "what do you do?" By every external measure (chapter 3 has informed you which scoreboard that is), it was going great.

I've already told you what it was from the inside — burnout by a thousand cuts, the phrase I put in print in 2015. But here's the part that belongs in *this* chapter: the cuts came from the *size* of the commitment, not the difficulty of the work. The work I could do all day; the work was the good part. What bled me was everything the bigness required — fighting for the right thing across nine stakeholders and compromising on schedule, every project, every time. The job kept getting *bigger* — bigger title, bigger projects — and the actual work, the part I was there for, kept getting smaller inside it. That's the ladder's con: every rung up converts doing into managing-the-conditions-for-doing. Nobody warns you that the view from the top of the ladder is mostly meetings about the ladder.

By the end of 2015 I was done. Not dramatic — done the way a battery is done.

And then I did the thing this chapter is about: I went *smaller*, on purpose, and it looked to everyone like a step down. I left the VP title and the private sector and took a job as a lead developer — note the direction: from VP back to *builder* — for municipal government. City of Boston. Union shop, civil service, fixed budgets, the approval chains you've heard me romanticize all book. On the résumé it read like a man pulling over. The conference-circuit guys would have called it quitting the game.

It was the best work of my life. I got to build the city's digital front door, with a small constrained team, for people who actually needed it — and in 2016 we open-sourced the whole thing, and other cities took what we built and built on it. A tiny team inside the most bureaucratic structure American employment offers, out-shipping the unconstrained. *That's* what was waiting downstairs from the ladder. The work itself, with the bigness scraped off.

I once wrote that the philosophy I found there was "developing with people, not for people." That sentence took me down a ladder to learn.

## Deep and narrow beats broad and thin.

So here's the chapter's claim, and then the gates: **small is not a compromise; small is where the work lives.** What feels like ambition — the packed portfolio, the seventeen simultaneous projects, the reply-all velocity — is mostly *surface area*, and surface area is where your week goes to die. The man spread across nine things isn't doing nine things; he's *administrating* nine things, doing none of them at the depth that either satisfies or distinguishes. Meanwhile the deep-and-narrow guy — the one with the uncluttered plate — does the two things that get remembered at review time. You've seen this man. Infuriating, isn't he? Leaves at five, owns two priorities, somehow indispensable. He's not working harder. He's *carrying less*, and the difference compounds (the tank chapter told you the exchange rate).

The gates, then — same hardware as the whole book, aimed at the office:

**A project cap.** A number, chosen in advance, of significant things you'll carry at once. Two, maybe three. The cap forces the clarifying question (chapter 5) at exactly the moment ambition would otherwise say yes: *if this comes in, what goes out?* No cap, and your plate becomes everyone else's junk drawer — every orphan project finds the man who can't say no, and his reward is more orphans.

**The professional no.** Practice the sentence: "I can't take that on and do it well." Notice it contains no apology and no maybe — "let me see if I can squeeze it in" is the trench-coat yes from chapter 8, and everyone knows it. Here's the secret about the workplace no that took me fifteen years to learn: *it reads as seniority.* The guy who declines gracefully is assumed to know what he's for. The guy who takes everything is assumed to be available because nothing he does matters much. Scarcity prices upward; your calendar is no exception.

**The courage to be unimpressive in meetings.** This one's subtle and it's my favorite. An enormous amount of career bigness is *performance* — visible busyness, theatrical urgency, volunteering in front of leadership, status updates engineered to sound load-bearing. It's the scoreboard (chapter 3), commuting to the office. Let it go. Be the guy with nothing dazzling to report and two finished things. Unimpressive in the meeting, undeniable in the work — that trade wins every year of a career except maybe the first two, and it hands you back astonishing amounts of attention you didn't know you were spending on theater.

## What walked back in the door.

And the dividend — because this chapter's recovery beat is the one I can testify to personally.

When work got smaller, the man who came home got bigger. That's not a greeting card; it's nearly mechanical, and you have all the parts now: the smaller job stopped draining the tank (11), freed the evenings the casino had been eyeing (9), un-cluttered the head that walks through the door. The thousand cuts had been billed, all those years, to my address — and when they stopped, what walked back in the door was *attention with no claims on it*. The follow-up question. The seventh minute. The dinner where I wasn't visibly somewhere else.

Your version doesn't require my résumé. Going smaller rarely means changing jobs; mostly it means the three gates above, run inside the job you have — and an honest hour with the question the printout audit asked of your calendar (chapter 8), aimed at your role: *how much of this would I choose again?* Strip the theater. Cap the plate. Decline like a senior. The career, counterintuitively, tends to *improve* — depth is rarer than effort and gets paid like it. But that's the side effect.

One move: this week, identify the one work commitment that exists purely because you never said no to it — the recurring meeting you haunt, the project you're "across" but not *on*, the committee. Exit it, using the sentence. No apology, no maybe.

Then notice what you do with the recovered hour — because that hour is the entire argument of Part III, and we're about to go there.
