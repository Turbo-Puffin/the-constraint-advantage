# 3. The scoreboard.

I used to keep score.

Not on purpose. Nobody sits down with a spreadsheet and decides to measure his life against the guy across the street. It just accumulates, the way junk mail does, until one day the mailbox won't close.

A guy you went to college with sells his company. A guy on your block redoes his kitchen and the island is the size of a Buick. A guy at school pickup mentions, casually, the way you'd mention the weather, that his kid is on three travel teams this fall. Three. And some lizard part of your brain, the part that has never once made you happy, writes it all down.

That's the scoreboard. You didn't build it. You didn't agree to it. You sure as hell didn't pick what it measures. But you check it every day, and it has an opinion about you every day, and the opinion is always the same: *behind.*

## The game nobody signed you up for.

Here's what I want you to notice about the scoreboard: every single thing on it was put there by someone else.

The square footage. The job title. The car that parks itself while the neighbors watch. The vacation that photographs well. The kid who plays three sports at a level that requires a second mortgage and a minivan with a roof box. None of these are your values. They're the *default* values, the factory settings, and you've been measuring your one finite life against a list of things you never actually chose.

I spent years doing this in my career. Consulting will do it to you fast, because consulting is a business where everyone is scored constantly: utilization, rate, the prestige of the client, who got staffed on the shiny project and who got the death march. I got good at that game. I won rounds of it. And I can tell you with the certainty of a man who has checked, there is no prize at the end. There is just the next round, with better players and a worse exchange rate on your hours.

Meanwhile, back at the house, there was a different scoreboard. I didn't look at that one nearly as often. It was kept by people much shorter than me, in a currency I was hemorrhaging without noticing: whether I was *there.*

That's the part that costs you. Not that comparison makes you feel bad. Who cares how you feel; you're a grownup. The cost is that the scoreboard sets your schedule. Every hour you spend climbing in the game you didn't choose comes out of the only game you'd actually cry about losing.

## Comparison is noise wearing a suit.

We treat envy like it's information. It isn't. It's noise that dresses well.

The guy with the exit? You're comparing your whole messy life to his press release. You didn't see the three years he didn't see his family, the cofounder he no longer speaks to, the stomach lining he donated to the cause. You're comparing your blooper reel to his trailer, and then feeling like garbage about the comparison, and then, this is the truly stupid part, *adjusting your life* to close the gap.

You take the bigger job you didn't want. You say yes to the project that eats your fall. You sign the kid up for the third team because the other dad's kid has three, and now your Saturdays belong to a parking lot in **[whatever town has the tournament]**, and everyone in the minivan is miserable, including the kid, who would have been thrilled with one team and a father who wasn't checking his phone at the games.

I'll say that part again, because it's the whole chapter: the scoreboard doesn't just make you feel behind. It *spends your time for you.* It is, functionally, a guy who has access to your calendar and hates your family.

## Nobody at your funeral reads the scoreboard.

Morbid? Sure. Useful? Extremely.

Picture the room. (You can picture it; you've been to a few of these by now. That's what your forties are: weddings get rarer and funerals pick up the slack.) Nobody stands up at the front and says square footage numbers. Nobody reads job titles into the record. The eulogy has never once included the phrase "utilization rate." Nobody announces the credit score, either. Eight hundred and four. He'd want you to know.

The people in the front row were keeping score the whole time, though. Oh, they kept *meticulous* score. They just used a different sheet. Was he there. Did he listen. Did he show up at the thing, and when he was at the thing, was he *at the thing,* or was he in the parking lot on a call.

The front row's scoreboard is the only one with your real name on it. Everything else is a participation trophy for a game that was rigged before you were born — rigged by people selling kitchens and minivans and travel leagues, who are, I want to be clear, *delighted* with how you've been spending your weekends.

## You can put it down.

Here's the good news, and it's the same good news as every chapter in this book: you don't have to win the game. You're allowed to stop playing.

Not by moving to a cabin. Not by becoming the guy who announces at the cookout that he's "off social" now, with the same energy as a man describing his juice cleanse (we'll get to that guy; he means well; he has replaced one scoreboard with a smugger one). You stop playing the way you fix everything else in this book: you choose your own scoreboard, on purpose, and you make it small.

Mine has about four things on it now. **[Matt's real four — or we write them together.]** Whether the work I do is worth doing. Whether the people in my house get the version of me that's actually awake. Whether I can take a Tuesday afternoon and not ask anyone's permission. Whether I'm proud of what I said no to. That's it. That's the whole league I compete in. Membership: one.

And here's the thing nobody tells you, the dirty secret of quitting the big game: the moment you stop measuring yourself against the neighbor's kitchen, the neighbor's kitchen becomes *just a kitchen.* You can stand in it at the block party and genuinely admire the island. It's a nice island. It holds no power over you. The man is just a man who likes countertops, and you are just a man who is going home at nine because Saturday morning belongs to pancakes, and both of you are fine.

Comparison only costs you something while you agree it counts.

So: one move, and it's a pencil-and-paper move, not a life overhaul. Tonight, write down what's actually on your scoreboard, the real one you've been checking, the one with the college roommate and the kitchen island on it. Look at the list. Circle anything on it you would defend out loud, to your wife, as a thing worth trading your evenings for.

You won't circle much. Most men circle nothing.

Then write the short list. The one with the front row on it.

Keep score of that.
