# 6. Money: the fixed budget.

Money fights aren't about money. You've noticed this. You're mid-fight about a $40 charge and some part of your brain, the part watching from the cheap seats, says: *we both know this isn't about the charge.*

It's about the ambient hum. In most houses, money isn't a topic, it's a *weather system* — a low-pressure front that sits over the kitchen permanently, ready to storm at the sight of an Amazon box. Nobody knows the actual numbers, which is the secret: the dread runs on vagueness. The tight jaw at dinner isn't about what you spent. It's about not knowing, on any given day, whether you're okay.

This chapter installs a number where the weather used to be.

## Vague money is expensive money.

Here's the mechanism, and notice it's the same one from chapter 5. A household without a fixed number runs every purchase as a fresh court case. Can we afford the camp? The car repair plus the camp? The vacation, given the camp, given the car? Every decision relitigates your entire financial life from scratch — and the two judges hearing the case are tired, it's 9 p.m., and each suspects the other of contempt.

That's the real cost of vague money. Not overspending — though, yes, also overspending. The cost is that *every dollar becomes a conversation*, and the conversations are all the same conversation, and the conversation never ends because it has no terms. You can't win a negotiation that has no number in it. You can only adjourn it, nightly, for thirty years.

I learned the alternative professionally before I ever applied it at home, which is backwards, but most of my good ideas arrived backwards. I spent years working inside government budgets — *actually* fixed budgets, the kind with laws around them. You'd think that would feel like a straitjacket. It felt like the opposite. When the number is truly the number, a strange peace breaks out: nobody burns energy on what the money *might* do. The fights — and there were fights — were about what mattered most *inside* the number. Which is the only money conversation that was ever worth having.

The fixed number doesn't restrict the conversation. It *upgrades* it. From "can we afford this?" — unanswerable, recurring, corrosive — to "what do we want most?" — answerable, occasional, and weirdly close to romantic.

## The conversation you have once.

So here's the move, and I'll be straight with you: it's one bad evening. One.

You and your wife, a kitchen table, the real numbers, and ninety minutes. What actually comes in. What actually has to go out. And then the decision — *the* decision, the one this chapter is named for: the number. What this household spends, monthly, full stop. Not a guideline. Not a vibe. A number with a wall around it.

**[STORY SLOT: the Crist version of this evening — when the number got set, what it changed. If it hasn't happened yet, this chapter is the assignment.]**

I won't pretend the evening is fun. Real numbers are confrontational; that's why everyone keeps them vague. Somebody's going to defend a subscription with genuine passion. (It's you. It's your subscription.) But watch what you're actually doing in that ninety minutes: every future money fight you were going to have at 9 p.m., tired and accusatory, you are having *now*, once, on purpose, calm, with coffee. You're not having an argument. You're having all of them, wholesale, at a steep discount.

And when it's done, something goes quiet in the house. The weather system breaks up. An Amazon box becomes just a box — it's either inside the number or it isn't, and the box doesn't *mean* anything anymore, doesn't carry a verdict about whether you're okay. You know whether you're okay. You decided it in January.

That's the part nobody tells you about budgets, because budget people are, historically, the least romantic people alive: **a fixed number is an act of love.** It takes the single largest source of low-grade marital static and converts it into a settled question. The money conversation happens once, on purpose — instead of forever, by accident.

## What the wall buys.

Two dividends, then the move.

First: inside the wall, spending gets *better*, not grimmer. This surprises everyone. When the number is fixed, every dollar inside it is pre-approved — guilt-free by construction. The constraint doesn't make you cheap; it makes you deliberate, and deliberate money buys better things. The camp, chosen on purpose, instead of the camp plus four hedges against feelings. Fewer, better — it's the whole book, denominated in dollars.

Second, and this is the regret-flavored one: the unfixed budget is what *funded* the going-too-hard years. Run the causality: vague money creates ambient dread; ambient dread whispers *more* — take the bigger job, the extra project, hour fifty-one through sixty-five — because when you don't know what enough is, no amount answers the question. **A man without a number can never earn his way to done.** That's how the lie of more (chapter 2) gets its financing: not by your greed — by your vagueness. I gave years to a dread that a single evening of arithmetic would have dissolved. The evenings I traded for it, I knew the price of. I just never checked what I was buying.

The number is how you find out what enough is. And *enough*, it turns out, is the load-bearing word in this entire book — because enough is where peace starts, and you cannot locate it without a wall to measure from.

One move, and you already know it: schedule the evening. This week. Ninety minutes, the real numbers, the table, both of you.

One bad evening against thirty years of weather. You'll never get a better exchange rate on anything.
