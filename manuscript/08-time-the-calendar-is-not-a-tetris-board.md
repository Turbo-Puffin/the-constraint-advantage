# 8. Time: the calendar is not a Tetris board.

Of all the chapters in this book, this is the one I wrote with my jaw tight.

Money comes back. You can re-earn money; I have. Stuff comes back, regrettably; the garage refills like a tide pool. But the resource in this chapter has no buyback program, no second market, no customer service line. A Tuesday from when your kid was eight is gone with a completeness that nothing else in your life can match. The physicists tell us time only runs one direction. Fathers didn't need the physicists.

So before the jokes — and there are jokes coming, this is still that kind of book — let me say the quiet part plainly: this is the chapter where the regret lives. I missed time I don't get back, and I missed it in the stupidest possible way. Not all at once, dramatically, like the dads in movies. A half hour here. A "you guys go ahead" there. **[STORY SLOT: the over-scheduled stretch — the season the calendar won. What it looked like, what finally got cut.]** Nobody steals your years in a heist. They get shoplifted, item by item, by a calendar you were proud of.

## The Tetris instinct.

Here's the disease, and you have it: somewhere along the way, you started treating an open slot on the calendar as a *problem*.

A free Saturday morning appears three weeks out, and your brain — trained by two decades of professional achievement — registers it as waste. An inefficiency. A gap into which something productive could be slotted. And oh, the candidates line up: the kid could do a clinic, you could knock out the gutters, there's that breakfast thing you got invited to which would be "good to do." The block descends, rotates, clicks into place. Very satisfying. The row clears.

That's Tetris. And the thing about Tetris — the actual game — is instructive: *there is no winning state.* You cannot finish Tetris. The reward for placing blocks well is faster blocks. The game only ends when you lose. A man playing calendar Tetris is optimizing his own burial, four weeks at a time, and his high score is a eulogy that says "he was so busy."

The instinct comes from a category error: treating time like money. Money rewards deployment — idle money really is wasted money, ask anyone with savings in a checking account. So the competent man deploys his hours the same way, gets them all *working*. But time doesn't compound when deployed. Time compounds when *attended*. An hour deployed three ways is worth less than an hour spent entirely on one thing — and the inputs that matter most run on the one schedule you can't book: a kid doesn't open up at the scheduled bonding slot, 9:00–9:45 Saturday. He opens up in the seventh minute of nothing in particular, in a car, sideways, when no transition to anything is looming. **Unscheduled time isn't the gap between the important things. It's the load-bearing wall.** Knock it out and the important things fall into each other, which is why your "full" seasons are somehow also your worst ones.

## The standing no and the seasonal yes.

Now the gates. Two of them — and notice neither requires becoming a monk; the calendar stays a calendar. It just acquires walls.

**The standing no.** The default answer to new recurring commitments is no — not "let me check," which is a yes wearing a trench coat. No is the gate; exceptions need to *earn* the key. (You've heard "if it's not a hell yes it's a no" from the productivity guys. Fine, same hardware — but they aim it at output. Aim it at home: things compete for your hours; the hours belong to the front row; act like it.) The trick is deciding the policy in advance — chapter 5, decide once — because case-by-case, you lose. Every invitation is reasonable. Every one. The breakfast thing is *fine*, the committee is *worthy*. Nothing on your calendar got there by being obviously bad; it got there by being individually defensible, and the aggregate is how a man ends up with a fall schedule he'd never have chosen and can't remember choosing. You don't fight the aggregate retail. You fight it wholesale, with policy.

**The seasonal yes.** For the kids' stuff, the rule in our house is capacity-shaped: one sport at a time, per kid, per season — pick activities like you pick what fits in the trunk, because the trunk is the family's actual bandwidth and *everyone* rides in it. (The travel-team arms race, you'll recall, lives on someone else's scoreboard. Chapter 3 already told you whose.) Here's the secret about the kids, by the way — the one the league email lists don't want circulating: the kid with three activities isn't getting three childhoods. He's getting zero, plus logistics. Kids don't experience your heroic shuttling as abundance. They experience the van. Ask the kid — actually ask — and the data is one team, full dad. It's not close.

**The empty slot, defended.** And what the two gates protect is the white space itself. Here's the reframe that finally made it stick for me: that empty Saturday block *already has a meeting in it.* The attendee list is just shorter than you're used to — her, mainly. The meeting agenda is nothing. Pancakes, optionally. And when something asks to book over it, treat it exactly like you'd treat someone trying to book over the CEO: with confusion that they asked. Because run the actual seniority chart of your life — who's the meeting *with*? She's twelve. The math on how many Saturdays are left at twelve is public information, and it is not in your favor.

## The audit.

One move this week, pencil-and-paper division.

Print the last full month. Sit with a highlighter and mark every block that was a *choice* — yours, current, would-choose-it-again. Leave unmarked everything that's there by momentum: inherited commitments, undead recurring meetings, obligations to people you don't owe, the breakfast things.

Most men highlight less than half. Sit with that. You wouldn't run a business where half the payroll was historical accident — and this *is* the payroll. It's the one you're spending down with no buyback, the one denominated in someone's childhood.

Then cancel one unmarked thing. Just one; this book doesn't do bonfires. Don't replace it. Let the slot stand empty all month, weird and unproductive and yours, and watch what wanders into it.

Something always wanders in. It's usually short, and it usually wants pancakes.
