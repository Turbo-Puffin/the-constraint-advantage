# 12. Leverage: make the machines do the boring parts.

This is the chapter where I'm supposed to sell you on AI, and I want to start by lowering your expectations, because everyone else selling it to you is doing the opposite.

You've heard the pitch. AI will 10x your output. AI will let you do *more* — more content, more projects, more hustle per hour. Every one of those pitches is the lie of more (chapter 2) with a software license — the same scoreboard, the same loop, now with a subscription fee. Watch where the pitch aims: at your *output*. Nobody selling productivity software has ever once asked what the freed-up hour was *for*. They assume you'll reinvest it in more output, and the grim joke is, left unsupervised, you will.

So here's the reframe, and it's this book's only piece of technology advice: **the machines are for doing less.** Not 10x your output — 10x your *evenings*. Same lever, opposite direction. It turns out the most radical thing you can do with the most hyped technology of the decade is point it at the boring parts of your life and then *keep the time.*

## The listing.

Let me make it concrete, because abstract AI talk is how the hype guys keep you nodding.

You'll recall we're emptying the house (chapter 7). The purge generates a constant stream of stuff worth selling — and selling stuff, every dad knows, is one of the great boring time-eaters of domestic life. Photograph the thing, write the listing, research what it's worth, post it, answer the "is this still available" messages from people who will never buy it, haggle, arrange pickup. Call it forty-five minutes per object, scattered across a week in attention-fragments (chapter 9 told you what those cost). Which is why every garage in America contains a pile labeled, mentally, *sell eventually* — a pile that is, functionally, a part-time job nobody ever clocks into.

Here's what happens to that pile in my house now: I take a picture of the thing. That's it — that's my whole job in this story. The agent does the rest: figures out what it is, what it's worth, writes the listing, posts it, fields the tire-kickers, and tells me when it's sold and what to do next. The boring forty-five minutes doesn't get *optimized*. It stops existing. The pile in the garage converts to money and space while I'm doing something else — and "something else," on a good evening, is nothing at all, on purpose, with people I like.

Notice the shape of that, because the shape is the whole chapter: the machine took over a *chore*, end to end, and the dividend got paid out in *time*, and the time went to the front row. Nobody 10x'd anything. A Tuesday evening got bought back, and we know from chapter 9 exactly whose Tuesday that was.

## The receipt.

Now, the part where I establish this lever is real and not a gadget, because your skepticism is warranted and I'd like to spend it down with evidence.

I run my working life this way. Companies — plural, small, real — operated essentially alone, with agents doing the work that org charts were invented for: the bookkeeping-shaped things, the monitoring, the routine correspondence, the boring middle of everything. My AI bill is a few hundred dollars a month — less than most families spend on groceries — doing work I'd otherwise hire for, badly, at salaries with commas in them. I say this not to recruit you into entrepreneurship (chapter 13 has gentler plans for your career) but as a *load test*: if the lever holds up running entire businesses, it will hold up running your garage sale.

But here's the part of the receipt that actually belongs in this book: what the machines bought me isn't scale. I could have aimed all that leverage at *more* — more companies, more output, the 10x life; the machinery would have obliged. The constraint (chapter 5, decide once) was pointing it at *enough* — the same work, the same modest empire, run in radically fewer hours, with the surplus paid out in present-tense fatherhood instead of growth. The machines made my businesses quieter, not bigger. That was the whole point. The hype guys aim the lever at the scoreboard. Aim it at the kitchen.

## The dashboard rule.

One guardrail before you go shopping, and it's the only one you need, so I'll give it a box of its own:

**If the tool adds a dashboard you have to check, it failed.**

That's the test, and it's binary, like the notification rule (chapter 9: people bark, apps don't). The whole transaction was *attention for money* — you're buying back hours. A tool that needs babysitting — a dashboard to monitor, settings to fiddle, streaks to maintain, weekly reports to feel guilty about — hasn't removed a chore; it's *hired you to manage one*, and now you're the unpaid supervisor of a robot, which is somehow a worse job than the chore. (The smart-home guys know exactly who they are here. There is a man on your street who spends four hours a week maintaining the automations that save him forty minutes a week. There's a German word for him, probably; the English one is *hobbyist*, and that's fine, but it's a hobby, not leverage.)

Real leverage is silent. The agent that sells the garage pile passes the test the way a good employee does: it tells you when something's *done* or when it genuinely needs you, and otherwise it shuts up. Apply that bar to everything in this category — the bill-watcher, the email-triager, the scheduler — and you'll throw most of it back, which is correct. This chapter isn't pro-gadget. It's pro-*chore-death*, and the distinction is the dashboard.

And the same bar applies to the brochure version of this chapter, so let me say it plainly: if the machines have you *doing more*, they failed too. The technology is genuinely good now; that part of the hype is real. Which makes the direction you point it the entire game. Output is a scoreboard (chapter 3), and you know whose. Point the lever home.

One move: pick the single most boring recurring chore in your house — the one you'd pay a teenager twenty bucks to make disappear. Find the agent-shaped tool that kills it end to end (the garage pile is an excellent first candidate), run the dashboard test ruthlessly, and when it works — this is the actual assignment — *spend the recovered time on purpose.* Not on the next chore. The dividend goes to the front row, or the lever's just the lie of more with better marketing.

The machines are finally good enough to do the boring parts.

Let them. You have somewhere to be.
