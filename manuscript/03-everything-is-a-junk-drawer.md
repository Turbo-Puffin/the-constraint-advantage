# 3. Everything is a junk drawer.

The junk drawer is the most honest square foot in your house.

Everything else is staged. The living room performs for guests. The office performs for video calls. Even the garage is lying, technically, because the story of the garage is "I'll get to it," and the garage knows you won't.

But the junk drawer tells the truth. Open it. Go ahead, it's the one to the left of the silverware, it fights back a little because a spatula is wedged against a flashlight that needs a kind of battery they stopped making. Inside: keys to nothing. A phone charger for a phone that lives in a landfill now. Seventy cents, a birthday candle, a cable tie, three pens (one works), and a warranty card for a blender you're not sure you still own.

Here is what that drawer actually is. It's not a mess. It's a pile of decisions you didn't make. Every object in there arrived at a moment when somebody, and I want to be delicate here, it was you, held a thing in their hand, didn't know what it was for, couldn't prove it was for nothing, and put off the call. The drawer is where verdicts go to not happen.

One drawer of deferred decisions is fine. Every house gets one. That's not the problem.

The problem is that the drawer is not a drawer. It's a blueprint. It is the organizing principle of your entire life, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it, and I'm about to ruin your Tuesday to prove it.

## One ordinary Tuesday.

The alarm goes off at 5:45 and the phone is already in your hand, which is funny, because you don't remember asking it to be there. Before your feet touch the floor you have triaged fourteen notifications. Triaged, not handled. Three you dismiss, one you feel bad about, and ten you leave sitting there, glowing, for later. You have opened the day's first junk drawer and it isn't 6 a.m. yet.

The inbox is next. Two hundred and some unread, but that number stopped meaning anything years ago. You're not reading email, you're patrolling it. Somewhere in there is a renewal notice for software you don't use, a newsletter you meant to unsubscribe from during a different presidency, and four threads you're cc'd on for reasons no one alive can explain. You handle none of it. You star two things. Starring is putting it in the drawer.

The calendar. Look at Thursday. Who is that call with? You genuinely don't know. It recurs. It has always recurred. Somewhere in the past a version of you said "sure" to something, and that "sure" has been billing you thirty minutes a week ever since, like a gym membership for a gym that doesn't exist. The rest of the week is no better: a standing thing you attend so nobody talks, a "quick sync" that has never once been quick, a Saturday obligation you agreed to in a weak moment at a cookout in, you believe, 2019.

The group texts fire up around eight. There are five of them. You would pay real money to leave two, but leaving a group text in the suburbs is read as a declaration of war, so instead you send the thumbs-up emoji, which is the "sure" of the modern age. Into the drawer.

Somewhere around lunch, the phone offers you a story about a conflict on the other side of the world, and you read it, and then a take about the story, and then a take about the take. You now hold opinions that will be obsolete by Friday about people you will never meet. You needed none of it. It cost you forty minutes and a little bit of your mood. You'd unsubscribe, but there's no button for the whole world.

And underneath all of it, all day, the quiet hum: the deck that needs staining. The dentist thing you keep not scheduling. The conversation with your brother you keep not having. The someday-project, the someday-trip, the someday-shape you'll get in. None of it is happening and none of it is cancelled. It's all just open. At 2 a.m., when you'd very much like to be asleep, your head pulls the drawer out and dumps it on the bed and makes you look.

That's one Tuesday. Count the drawers: the phone, the inbox, the calendar, the group texts, the news, the head, and we haven't even walked into your actual garage, which is the flagship location. It's the same drawer everywhere. Different sizes, same physics. Things kept without a decision. Verdicts postponed. "Sure" said once and billed forever.

## Clutter bills you.

Here's the mechanism, and it's the most important paragraph in this book, so I'll keep it short.

Everything you keep makes a claim on you. Not a big one. A tiny one. The unfinished project claims a flicker of guilt every time you walk past it. The recurring meeting claims Thursday. The group text claims a thumbs-up. The unread pile claims that little exhale you do when you see the badge count. Each claim is nothing. A rounding error. You'd never even bother writing it down.

But nobody owns one claim. You own thousands, and they compound, and that is the whole trick of it: **clutter is chaos on a payment plan.** No single payment will ever hurt enough to force a decision, and the total is quietly bankrupting you. Not your wallet. Your attention, your evenings, your patience at 6 p.m. when a kid asks you a question and gets the flat version of you that answers without looking up.

You keep waiting for the big thing that's wrong with your life to reveal itself, some dramatic villain you can finally punch. There isn't one. There is no wolf. It's ten thousand mosquitoes, and each one is billing you a dollar a month, and you gave them your card number the day you said "sure," or clicked "subscribe," or set the box in the basement instead of deciding what was in it.

I didn't figure this out in an armchair. My wife and I have been emptying our house, actually emptying it, room by room, a running project and not a weekend stunt, and the thing I did not expect was what it did to my head. The first cleared room went quiet in a way I could feel in my chest before I could explain it. The room had been billing me. I'd been paying a tax on that room for years, in glances and guilt and a low-grade sense of being behind, and I only found out when the payments stopped. And then, because I am apparently slow, it took me another few weeks to see that my calendar was the same room. And my phone was the same room. And my Thursday.

The stuff was never the point. The stuff was just the version of the disease I could see.

## You cannot organize your way out.

Now, the trap. Because the moment a guy like you sees this, and you do see it now, I watched you count the drawers, your instinct is to get organized. Buy the bins. Label the shelves. Install the app that sorts the inbox into clever folders, download the calendar tool with the color coding, adopt a System with a capital S from a guy on the internet whose entire life is the System.

I did the consulting years. I have seen more Systems than a NASA subcontractor. Here is what a beautifully organized bin of things you never use actually is: a monument to never-use, just easier to dust. Organizing is not deciding. Organizing is the junk drawer with a seating chart. The claims don't stop when you sort them, they just show up in matching containers, and now the containers bill you too.

The color-coded calendar full of meetings that shouldn't exist is not a fixed calendar. It's a crime scene with good lighting. The inbox app that files your unread mail into tidy folders has not answered one goddamn email. It's built you a filing cabinet for your postponed decisions, and it charges $9.99 a month, which, note the shape of that: you are now paying a bill to store your bills.

More containers is just more. And "more" is the thing that got you here, dressed up in an apron and calling itself help.

## Subtraction, by rule.

The only move that works is the one nobody wants: fewer things making claims. Not sorted. Fewer. The decision you kept deferring, made. The verdicts, handed down. Keep or kill, and everything that doesn't clearly earn "keep" gets killed, and I mean everything: the objects, the meetings, the subscriptions, the standing obligations, the group texts, the inputs, the someday-projects. The whole estate.

And because you cannot possibly make ten thousand individual verdicts (you'd die in the garage around verdict ninety, litigating a kayak), you don't make them one at a time. You make rules that decide for you, in advance, wholesale. One rule kills a hundred claims and you never hear from them again. That's a constraint. The last chapter of this part is about how they work and why they hold when willpower folds like a lawn chair.

Then the middle of this book walks the house with you, room by room, and the rooms are your life: the stuff, the money, the calendar, the screen, the head, the circle of people, the tank you run on, the work. Same disease in every room. Same move in every room. Name the clutter, set the rule, collect the quiet.

That's the whole book. I'd tell you it's more complicated, but I'm trying to quit lying to you, that's chapter two.

## The drawer, again.

So, tonight, after everyone's asleep: open the drawer. The real one, by the silverware.

Take everything out. Put it on the counter. Put back the five things that have a job (the working pen made the cut, congratulations to the working pen), and let the rest go. Actually go. Trash, donate, gone. It'll take eleven minutes, and roughly six of those will be you holding the mystery keys up to the light like a jeweler, trying one last time to remember the locks. You won't. They were never going to open anything again. You weren't keeping keys. You were keeping the not-deciding.

Then close the drawer and open it once more, just to feel it. Nothing shifts. Nothing fights back. Nothing in there is waiting for you.

Now stand in your kitchen and be honest about what you're feeling, because it isn't pride and it isn't tidiness. It's quiet. One square foot of your life just stopped billing you.

You own a lot of square feet. Some of them are drawers, some of them are Thursdays, and some of them are the hour before you fall asleep.

We're going to get them all back.
