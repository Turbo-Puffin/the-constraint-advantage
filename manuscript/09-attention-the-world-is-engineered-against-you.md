# 9. Attention: the world is engineered against you.

I spent a lot of years building the internet, so take this as expert testimony: the internet is not your friend.

I don't mean that the way your uncle means it. I mean it professionally. I've sat in rooms where attention products get designed, and I can report that the people building them are smart, well paid, extremely well rested — and after a career watching the industry from the inside, I wrote an entire furious essay about the patterns it ships on purpose. The cancellation flow built like a hedge maze. The "retention" team whose actual job title should be *department of making it extremely fucking difficult to leave.* The defaults that auto-renew, auto-play, auto-enroll — every path of least resistance professionally paved toward their door.

That was the *old* internet, mind you. The subscription stuff. Quaint. The new stuff doesn't want your credit card; it wants the card's owner. The feed is a slot machine that pays out in outrage and other people's vacations; the notification system is a trained dog that has learned exactly which barks make you reach for your pocket. None of this is a moral failing on your part. **You are a man in a casino that was designed by ten thousand engineers, and you walked in carrying the one thing they bill in: the last free hour of your day.**

## Whose hour was that, though.

Here's the part that took me embarrassingly long to register, and it's the reason this chapter is in the book.

When the feed eats an hour, the accounting feels private. *My* time, my scroll, my vice — like a cigarette out back. But run the actual ledger of a father's day. The job gets its ten hours. The commute, the errands, the maintenance of the corporeal estate — by the time the day's mandatory invoices are paid, what's left is a sliver. An hour, maybe two, of *discretionary you*. That sliver is the entire daily budget for: your marriage, your kids, your own head.

That's what's in play at the casino. Not "your free time" — there's no such line item. The sliver belongs to the front row; it was theirs before the phone got to it. So when the phone takes it, the phone is not taking it from *you*.

And they watch it happen. That's the image I can't shake loose, the one that finally beat the habit out of me — not the wasted time but the *witnessed* waste. **[STORY SLOT: the moment Matt caught himself — or got caught. There's always a specific one.]** A kid who wants your attention and sees the phone get it instead is running a simple experiment with a clean result. Kids can't read minds. They read *faces*, and a face lit from below, thumb moving, eyes middle-distance — they know exactly what that face is doing. It's choosing.

You'd never say it out loud. You don't have to. You're saying it in the only language they fully believe.

## Counter-engineering.

Good news: this is the most fixable chapter in the book, because the enemy has a known shape. You can't out-discipline a casino — chapter 5 told you why; their engineers work full-time and your willpower works part-time. But you don't need discipline. You need *counter-engineering*. They use defaults against you; defaults work for whoever sets them. So set them.

**Give the phone a bedtime.** It sleeps in the kitchen. Not your nightstand — the kitchen, plugged in, like an appliance, which is what it is. This one rule, hung once (chapter 5), quietly returns both ends of the day: the night scroll and the morning scroll, which were bleeding the two hours your household is most likely to actually want you. The alarm clock argument is dead; alarm clocks cost twelve dollars and don't come with a casino attached.

**Strip the slot machine.** Every notification off except humans. Not "fewer." Off. The bank's marketing department does not get to bark in your pocket; the news does not get to bark — the news has not contained a same-day actionable item for you since the Cuban Missile Crisis, and you weren't born. If a thing matters, a person will tell you. That's the test, and it's binary: *people bark, apps don't.*

**Make the feeds appointment-only.** Delete them from the phone — the accounts can live on a computer, a thing you visit on purpose, sitting down, like the office the internet used to be. The phone keeps tools and loses casinos. (You'll relapse. Fine — reinstall for the trip, delete after. This is architecture, not religion; chapter 18 holds your spot.)

**And kill the path-of-least-resistance defaults in the house, while you're in there:** autoplay off everywhere, the TV doesn't pre-warm the next episode, the tablet lives in a drawer with its own bedtime. You are the sysadmin of this household. Act like one.

None of this is a digital detox, a phrase I'd like retired — detox is an event, and events end (the juice-cleanse guy from chapter 3 sends his regards, from his phone). This is just *defaults, chosen by the resident* instead of by the casino. The slot machine still exists. It just doesn't live in your pocket anymore, and the dog has been retrained to bark only at people.

## What wanders back in.

I'll tell you what you actually get back, because "screen time reduction" undersells it criminally.

You get boredom back. I'm serious — that specific, restless, standing-in-the-kitchen-at-8:40 feeling that the phone has been eating for fifteen years. It's uncomfortable for about a week. And then, because attention abhors a vacuum, it starts *wandering*, and wandering attention is where everything good in my life has ever come from. It wanders to the garage project. It wanders to the kid doing homework at the counter, and asks an idle question, and gets — seventh minute, sideways, the car rule from chapter 8 holds indoors too — an actual answer.

The casino was never billing you for time. It was billing you for *presence*, in hourly installments. Take the hour back and the people it belonged to notice faster than you'd believe. They always knew where it was going. They were reading your face.

One move tonight, and you already know which one: the phone sleeps in the kitchen.

Hang that single gate and the rest of this chapter is negotiable. The casino can have its lights and its noise. It just can't have the last hour.

That one was never yours to give.
